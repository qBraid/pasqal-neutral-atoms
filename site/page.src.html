Programming matter with light

0Primer

Three ideas, and you can read every plot below

An atom array is controlled by a handful of numbers. There are no gates here, only a laser whose strength and frequency you shape over a few microseconds.

Atoms in tweezers

Rubidium atoms sit in optical tweezers on a fixed, calibrated grid: a triangular lattice 5 µm apart. You choose which traps are filled. FRESNEL_CAN1 only accepts registers drawn from its calibrated layouts.

One global laser

The Rabi frequency Ω drives each atom between ground |g⟩ and a Rydberg state |r⟩. The detuning Δ sets how much the Rydberg state is favoured, and it plays the role of a magnetic field. One pulse shape drives all the atoms at once.

The blockade

Two Rydberg atoms repel with U = C₆/r⁶. Inside the blockade radius, where U ≫ Ω, two neighbours can't both be excited. That one constraint creates entanglement, magnetic order and graph constraints.

1The Rydberg blockade

Two atoms can share one excitation, but never hold two

We put three experiments into one register and drive them all with one resonant pulse: a pair 5 µm apart (A), a pair 10 µm apart (B), and a lone atom (C). Then we make the pulse longer, shot by shot.

The lone atom flops between |g⟩ and |r⟩ with period 2π/Ω = 1 µs. Pair B is too far apart to notice each other and behaves like two lone atoms. Pair A is inside the blockade radius, so it can only ever hold one excitation, shared in the entangled state (|gr⟩ + |rg⟩)/√2. That collective state oscillates √2 times faster.

2Growing an antiferromagnet

Sweep the laser, and a crystal appears

Seven atoms in a row, 5 µm apart. We turn on Ω and sweep Δ from negative, where every atom prefers the ground state, to positive, where every atom wants to be excited.

Neighbours can't both be excited, so the compromise is a Néel crystal: every other atom excited, 1010101. In spin language (Rydberg = ↑) this is an antiferromagnet, the Z₂-ordered phase seen on 51 atoms by Bernien et al. (2017). Done slowly, the sweep follows the ground state into the ordered phase. This is adiabatic state preparation, the workhorse of analog quantum simulation.

3A magnet made of light

Frustration, and the ⅓ magnetization plateau

On a triangular lattice the blockade rule can't be satisfied everywhere: on every triangle, at most one atom can be excited. The best the atoms can do is excite one of three sublattices, so exactly ⅓ of them are excited.

In magnet language this is a magnetization plateau at m = −⅓. Over a wide range of field (detuning), the magnetization stops changing. This is the triangular-lattice Ising antiferromagnet, the model behind the rare-earth crystal TmMgGaO₄. We use a 3×4 rhombus of 12 atoms, chosen so that the largest blockade-respecting pattern holds exactly 4 atoms: ⅓ of 12.

Twelve atoms is the free emulator's limit. The same physics at 49 atoms needs Pasqal's tensor-network emulator EMU_MPS (billed per minute) or the QPU itself. 4Solving a graph problem

The blockade is a constraint solver

Put atoms on the vertices of a graph so that the blockaded pairs are exactly its edges. "As many Rydberg atoms as possible, with no two neighbours both excited" is then the Maximum Independent Set problem.

MIS is NP-hard in general and turns up in scheduling, wireless network design and portfolio selection. A slow sweep prepares the ground state, and the ground state is the MIS. This is the approach of Ebadi et al. (Science, 2022). How slow is slow enough? We vary the sweep time.

→Run it yourself

Install once, run on the emulator, then the QPU

Everything on this page comes from four notebooks in one qBraid project. Installing it copies the repository, installs the Pasqal (Pulser) environment with its kernel, and adds the pasqal-on-qbraid skill for coding agents. You don't need a Pasqal account; your qBraid login is enough.

1

Install the project

Launch on qBraid, or in qBraid Lab open Explore → Projects → Neutral Atoms on qBraid. Choose Install, or Start with an agent to have an agent walk you through it.

2

Run a notebook

Open any notebook in ~/projects/pasqal-neutral-atoms/notebooks, pick the kernel Python 3 [Pasqal], and run all cells. TARGET = "emu" is free and takes about a minute.

3

Go to hardware

The last cell of each notebook prints a cost estimate and submits the same sequences to FRESNEL_CAN1 only when you set CONFIRM_QPU = True.

What the QPU cells cost

Prices are in qBraid credits (1 credit = USD 0.01) at FRESNEL_CAN1's current credits per minute of QPU time. They are estimated from one measured run, where 10 shots used 12 s. Queue time isn't billed. A sequence that Pasqal would reject is caught before submission and costs nothing.

Honest notes

  • These are demonstrations, not a quantum advantage. At 12 atoms a laptop solves every problem here exactly, and chapter 4 times it. What carries over to 100 atoms is the method.
  • The emulator is noiseless. On hardware, expect a few percent of preparation and detection error per atom, so crystal probabilities will be lower than shown.
  • The ⅓ plateau is a finite-cluster version of the triangular Ising antiferromagnet. Twelve atoms show the plateau but not the thermodynamic phase transition.
